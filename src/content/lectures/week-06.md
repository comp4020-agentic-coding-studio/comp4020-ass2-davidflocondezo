Every ascetic discipline shares a structure: find the point where the body
says stop, and keep going past it on purpose. This week's discipline works
past a specific, recognisable moment — eye strain, a flicker of boredom, a
felt "enough" — where a scroll would ordinarily end.

The loop underneath that moment has a specific structure in behavioural
psychology: a cue (boredom, a notification, an unlocked phone), a routine
(open the app, scroll), and a reward that is variable rather than fixed —
most scrolls turn up nothing memorable, but occasionally one turns up
something genuinely funny, upsetting, or validating. Rewards that arrive on
a variable schedule train a stronger, harder-to-extinguish habit than
rewards that arrive every time, which is the same mechanism that makes slot
machines compelling and most feeds compelling by design — a fixed reward
can be predicted and stopped for; a variable one can't.

Interrupting the loop works by adding friction at a specific point, not by
relying on willpower to override a reward that's already anticipated.
Removing the cue — notifications off, the app off the home screen — is more
effective than resisting the routine once it's started, because the loop is
easiest to break before it begins, not partway through it.

## How to scroll past your limit on purpose

1. **Name your actual cue.** Most scrolling starts from a specific
   trigger — a lock-screen notification, an idle hand, a bad feeling — not a
   decision; you can't interrupt a loop you haven't identified.
2. **Add friction at the cue, not the reward.** Logging out, deleting the
   app icon, or greyscaling the screen all raise the cost of starting the
   routine, which is far more effective than trying to stop mid-scroll once
   a reward has already landed.
3. **Set a rule that defeats variability, not a fixed limit.** A time limit
   competes with a variable-reward system and loses; a rule like "close
   after the third boring post in a row" targets the actual mechanism
   instead.
4. **Track it without judgement.** Behavioural loops respond to accurate
   feedback, not guilt — a simple count of sessions is more useful for
   breaking one than resolving to feel bad about it.
5. **Treat a broken streak as data, not failure.** The loop is designed to
   survive willpower; redesigning the friction after a lapse is the actual
   discipline, not white-knuckling the next attempt.
