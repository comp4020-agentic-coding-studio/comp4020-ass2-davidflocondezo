Apocryphal and pseudepigraphal texts were never rejected for being poorly
written — some are extraordinary. They were rejected for lacking provenance:
attributed to an author who didn't write them, claiming a lineage they don't
have. That's the exact failure mode of AI slop. It borrows every surface
feature of sincere, well-sourced writing — confident tone, moral clarity,
plausible detail — while being traceable to no author, no lived experience, no
event.

That makes slop worth studying on its own terms rather than dismissing. A
skilled forger has to understand canon well enough to imitate its form
convincingly; producing a piece of slop that reads as scripture, and then
being able to say precisely which formal features it's borrowing and which
provenance it lacks, is this week's actual test — not "can you make slop,"
which anyone can, but "can you show your work."

## Outline

- apocrypha as a provenance failure, not a quality failure
- what AI slop borrows from canon's form, and what it can't
- Apocryphal Composition: forging the form, then showing which parts are forged
