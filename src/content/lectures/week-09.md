No saint declares themselves one. Sainthood is conferred retroactively, by a
community that keeps venerating someone after the fact — which is exactly
how virality works, and exactly why neither can be manufactured by the
person it happens to.

Virality's actual mechanism is a threshold effect, not steadily accumulating
popularity. Most posts get a small, fairly predictable amount of initial
reach from a creator's existing audience, and the overwhelming majority stop
there. The small fraction that "go viral" cross a specific threshold —
typically when the post reaches people whose own networks don't already
overlap with the original audience — after which each new viewer reaches a
genuinely new pool rather than a re-covered one, and growth briefly turns
exponential instead of linear. That's why virality looks unpredictable from
inside a single post's own performance: the metrics look nearly identical
right up until the moment it crosses.

What makes veneration repeatable afterward is the icon: a single gesture,
sound, or image compressed enough to carry recognition that the full body of
work would otherwise take much longer to establish. That's a real
constraint on what can become iconic at all — a signature has to be
reproducible by other people, since imitation is what actually spreads it,
and distinguishable enough from the generic version of the same gesture that
the imitation still points back to the original. Most creators who set out
to build a "brand" fail at exactly this step: what they pick is either too
generic to be attributable or too elaborate to be copied.

## How to build (or spot) an icon

1. **Reduce, don't add.** An icon is a subtraction problem — find the
   smallest gesture, phrase, or visual element that's still attributable,
   not the most elaborate one.
2. **Make it reproducible.** If other people can't do a rough version of it
   themselves, it can't spread by imitation, which is how most icons
   actually propagate.
3. **Repeat it past the point of boredom.** Recognisability needs exposure
   well past the point where it feels stale to the person doing it — by the
   time you're tired of it, it's usually just becoming legible to a new
   audience.
4. **Watch for the network threshold, not the daily numbers.** A flat
   performance right up until a sudden jump usually means the post finally
   reached a non-overlapping audience, not that anything about the post
   changed.
5. **Separate popularity from canonization.** A post can cross the threshold
   once and disappear; canonization is being re-cited and re-recognised well
   after the original spike, which is a slower and different test.
