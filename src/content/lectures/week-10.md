A prophecy is a claim about a crisis or a reveal that hasn't happened yet,
and what separates a false prophet from a true one was never how
confidently the claim was delivered — both deliver with total confidence.
It's whether the claim survives contact with the event it predicted.

This isn't just an analogy — it's a documented finding. Large-scale studies
of how true and false stories spread on social platforms (most notably
Vosoughi, Roy and Aral's 2018 study of over 100,000 contested stories on
Twitter) found that false stories reached far more people, and spread
faster and deeper through the network, than true ones — and that novelty
and emotional arousal, especially fear, disgust, and surprise, were the
strongest predictors of retweeting, not accuracy. People are, on average,
more likely to share something that feels new and provokes a strong feeling
than something that's merely correct, and false claims are cheaper to make
novel and provocative because they aren't constrained by what actually
happened.

Because urgency substitutes for evidence in a false prophecy, the useful
question is never "does this feel true" — a well-built false claim is
optimised to feel exactly that — but "what would this claim need to be
true, and can I check any of it right now." A specific, falsifiable claim
(a date, a number, a name) can usually be checked in the time it takes to
consider sharing it; a claim built entirely from vague inevitability
("this is happening," "they don't want you to know") is often unfalsifiable
by design, which is itself informative.

## How to evaluate a claim before you share it

1. **Locate the falsifiable part.** If a claim makes no specific, checkable
   prediction, that's not a strength — it's a sign it was built to survive
   scrutiny rather than invite it.
2. **Ask what would disprove it.** A claim its own believer can't imagine
   being wrong is functioning as identity, not information.
3. **Check who benefits from the urgency.** Fear and outrage are the two
   strongest documented predictors of sharing — a claim engineered to
   produce either should raise your evaluation bar, not lower it.
4. **Wait for a second, independently-arrived-at source.** One account
   repeated widely is still one source; a second account with no connection
   to the first, saying the same thing, is real corroboration.
5. **Notice your own urge to share immediately.** That urge is often the
   signal working as designed, not evidence the claim is important — a true
   claim can usually survive the ten minutes it takes to check it.
